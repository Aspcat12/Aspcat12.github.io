## 1. Problem and motivation (§1)

The field moved from handcrafted features (MFCC, filterbanks) → continuous SSL vectors (Wav2Vec2, WavLM, HuBERT) → discrete tokens. The pull toward discrete is the success of autoregressive LLMs over discrete text: tokens let audio join a language model, turn generation into **classification instead of regression**, and compress cheaply.

The push-back is that "discretization inevitably introduces information loss". The paper states the resulting split plainly: continuous features remain preferred for *understanding* (citing Tang et al. 2024, Chu et al. 2023), while discrete tokens are increasingly favoured for *generation*. Ideally tokens would keep phonetic content, linguistic content, speaker identity, emotion and other paralinguistic cues — but "there is still limited understanding of how much information is lost during tokenization across different tasks."

A terminological note from **footnote 2**, worth carrying into our thesis in spirit: in speech processing "semantic" does *not* mean semantic. Semantic tokens "are better described as **phonetic units** (Sicherman & Adi, 2023), as they generally lack semantic content". DASB keeps the word only for cross-domain consistency.

---

## 2. What is actually new (§2)

Prior discrete benchmarks **decode tokens back to a waveform and score the waveform**. DASB does not:

> "Reconstruction-based benchmarks can overestimate representation quality because a strong decoder may mask deficiencies in the tokens, especially since most training losses are applied to the decoder output."

The claim is properly bounded — generative tasks *do* need a waveform, so a **pretrained frozen decoder is attached at the final stage only** and is never part of downstream modelling. All modelling and evaluation happen in discrete token space.

The second novelty is coverage across three families and three domains, with per-configuration TPE tuning and multi-seed reporting because discrete tokens are far more hyperparameter-sensitive than continuous features.

**Note for later (§6-6):** this same section promises DASB will "analyze factors such as **codebook size** and downstream head design". It does not analyse codebook size.

**How the semantic tokens are built** — the part closest to our pipeline. Take a pretrained 24-layer SSL model (Wav2Vec2-large / WavLM-large / HuBERT-large); pick **six layers** — two low (1, 3), two middle (7, 12), two high (18, 23); run **k-means with V = 1000 on each layer**. Each clustered layer becomes one "codebook".

*(My inference, not stated by the paper: for semantic tokenizers **C is therefore a count of SSL layers, not of residual VQ stages** — which is why their #Q values are 2 and 6 rather than 2/8/32. It follows from §3.1 plus the #Q column of Table 2, but the paper never spells it out.)*

Worth pausing on: *the entire "semantic" family here is offline k-means over frozen features, with no learned codebook and no reconstruction loss anywhere.* Structurally the same move as our pose-prototype vocabulary.

### 3.2 Bitrate — **Eq. (1)**, the equation the checklist wants

> **bitrate = log₂V · C · R**  …(1)
> where **C** = number of codebooks, **V** = number of vectors per codebook (vocabulary), **R** = frame rate of codes per second.

The paper's framing:
- A single token stream is often insufficient for speech, so encoders emit **multiple parallel streams**, one per codebook.
- Bitrate is tuned **by changing C**, which "introduces a trade-off between efficiency and performance".
- To avoid the trivial finding that a tokenizer wins by spending more bits, every tokenizer is evaluated at **the lowest, the highest, and the authors' recommended** bitrate (default where no alternatives exist).

**Pass-3 check — does Eq. (1) reproduce the paper's own numbers?** §3.1 says EnCodec "supports multiple bitrates from 1.5 to 24 kbps". With V = 1024 (log₂ = 10), R = 75: C = 2 → 10 × 2 × 75 = **1 500 bps = 1.5 kbps**; C = 32 → **24 000 bps = 24 kbps**. Exact match at both ends. **Eq. (1) is verified and safe to cite.**

Bitrates for the rest (**my computation from Table 1 + Eq. (1); not printed in the paper** — label as derived if used; all rows independently recomputed in the audit):

| Tokenizer | V | R | C | bitrate (derived) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| EnCodec / DAC | 1024 | 75 | 2 / 8 / 32 | 1.5 / 6.0 / 24.0 kbps |
| SpeechTokenizer | 1024 | 50 | 2 / 8 | 1.0 / 4.0 kbps |
| Mimi | 2048 | 12.5 | 8 / 32 | 1.1 / 4.4 kbps |
| Discrete WavLM / HuBERT / W2V2 | 1000 | 50 | 2 / 6 | ≈1.0 / ≈3.0 kbps |
| SQ-Codec | 20000 | 50 | 4 | ≈2.9 kbps |
| WavTokenizer | 4096 | 75 | 1 | 0.9 kbps |

What this shows: DASB explores a **16× bitrate range within a single tokenizer at fixed V** (EnCodec/DAC, C = 2→32), and roughly **27× across the whole benchmark** (0.9 → 24 kbps, where V is *not* controlled). **Nowhere does it hold C and R fixed and move V.**

### 3.3 Downstream head (Eq. 2)

Tokens → per-codebook embedding lookup → MLP scores each codebook at each timestep → softmax over codebooks → weighted sum:

> z_{c,t} = f_emb(d_{c,t}) ,  a_{c,t} = exp(z_{c,t}) / Σ_{k=1..C} exp(z_{k,t}) ,  h_t = Σ_c a_{c,t} z_{c,t}  …(2)

All embeddings are 1024-d and **randomly initialised** for the headline results (Appendix E justifies this). Heads train end-to-end with the embedding and attention layers. SQ-Codec is the exception: with ~20 k tokens a learnable embedding table failed to converge, so they use a ternary-matrix approach, concatenating four 256-d group embeddings.

### 3.4 Decoder

Generative tasks only, always frozen. Acoustic and hybrid tokenizers use their built-in decoder. Semantic tokens use the **scalable HiFi-GAN vocoder** of Mousavi et al. 2024, pretrained on LibriSpeech-960h, accepting a variable number of multi-layer semantic tokens via **layer dropout** — which is what lets one vocoder serve both C = 2 and C = 6.

## 4. Experimental protocol (§4)

- Two hyperparameters tuned per configuration — **number of layers** and **learning rate** — with **TPE** (Watanabe 2023) via **Orion** (Bouthillier et al. 2021), **20 trials** each.
- **3 random seeds**, mean ± std reported.
- **Two downstream architectures per task**, described by the paper as "both shallow and deep models" (Table 8's ordering is not consistently shallow→deep — e.g. VoxCeleb runs ECAPA-TDNN then BiLSTM+Linear).
- Continuous baseline uses the **same downstream setup**, with **WavLM weighted-sum features unless otherwise specified**. The specified exceptions are BEATs (ESC-50), MERT (GTZAN genre), DEMUCS (MUSDB), TDCN++ (FUSS), Tacotron2-LibriTTS (multi-speaker TTS). §4.2 states explicitly that **speech separation uses the same architecture with WavLM weighted-sum** — so SE/SS/ASR *are* controlled representation comparisons; the five listed tasks are not.
- Compute: every task fits on ≥32 GB VRAM; KS ≈ 8 h, TTS ≈ 48 h on one A100.
- ASR text tokenisation: character vs 500-unit BPE for English, 100/200/500-unit BPE for the multilingual sets, best reported.
- TTS inference: 10 samples via top-K, then Whisper-Large picks the one best matching the text label. Appendix H grid-searches k ∈ {10, 20, 30} × temperature; the UTMOS effect is minor except at extremes, the **dWER effect is large and roughly convex in temperature** (most visible at k = 20). Appendix G notes multi-sampling does *not* rescue high-bandwidth configurations, indicating "a fundamental difficulty with predicting a large number of codebooks in each step rather than a simple issue of high sampling variability".

### 5.3 Music and general audio (Table 4) — **read the membership before the ranking**

⚠️ **DASB evaluates only the four acoustic tokenizers outside speech.** Tables 4 and 11 contain **EnCodec, DAC, SQ-Codec and WavTokenizer** plus the Continuous and Mixture reference rows. **No semantic and no hybrid tokenizer appears in either.** Figure 3 confirms it: the music/audio radar's rank axis runs **1–4** while the speech radar runs **1–9**.

Continuous: ESC **92.91**, USC **85.70**, MSC **100.00**, PC **90.60**, MGC **87.00**.
EnCodec at C = 2: ESC 34.83, USC **60.03**, MSC **100.00**, PC **69.80**, MGC **70.33**. Best discrete ESC is **EnCodec at C = 8, 37.00**.

So **there is no semantic-vs-acoustic "flip" outside speech** — EnCodec wins a **four-way, acoustic-only** comparison, at all bitrates and both domains, with DAC underperforming (the paper attributes that to DAC's emphasis on perceptual quality over signal fidelity). **The benchmark supplies no evidence at all about how speech-fitted k-means tokens behave on music.**

The ESC-50 gap, **92.91 → 37.00**, is the largest discrete/continuous gap in the paper *in absolute percentage points* (my computation, not a paper claim; in relative terms the ASR gap is worse). Part of it is baseline strength — the continuous ESC baseline is BEATs, a purpose-built audio model. Separation barely works for anyone: unprocessed mixtures sit at ≈ **−16 dB** SI-SDR for general audio and ≈ **−7.7 dB** for music, and the best model (EnCodec, medium bitrate) reaches only ≈ **−7 dB** and **−5.7 dB**.

### 5.4 The four ablation findings (§5.2)

**(a) Medium bitrate wins.** "Tables 2, 3, and 4 show that **medium bitrate settings achieve the best results** across both discriminative and generative tasks. Higher bitrates, while preserving more information, often degrade performance by increasing output dimensionality and modeling complexity." In RVQ models early codebooks carry more phonetic information and later ones "often add redundancy". Supporting citations: **Moshi** (Défossez et al. 2024) — lower bitrates can sometimes be better; **VoxtLM** (Maiti et al. 2024) — raising centroids **200 → 1000 worsens TTS CER from 3.5 to 6.1**.

**(b) Data scaling.** Discrete WavLM at low bitrate with a BiLSTM head: **6.0 % WER on LibriSpeech (960 h) → 22.0 % on Basque (116 h) → 58.9 % on Welsh (8 h)**. Acoustic tokenizers degrade far more sharply in low-resource settings; continuous features hold up best.

**(c) Model scaling.** Deeper heads rescue discrete tokens, acoustic ones most of all: **EnCodec goes from ~16 % WER with BiLSTM to ~8 % with Branchformer** (Table 9: 16.83 → 8.43 at C = 8). Same direction for IC (linear + statistical pooling → BiLSTM) and TTS (Tokotron → VALL-E). Under limited data, deep models often fail to converge on discrete tokens at all.

**(d) Stability.** High-bitrate acoustic models show the largest seed variance, especially with shallow heads. Large vocabularies are their own problem: SQ-Codec (~20 k tokens) "can still struggle to converge due to increased prediction complexity". Appendix D states that **Branchformer beats BiLSTM for every tokenizer except SQ-Codec**, blaming cross-entropy over a 20 k vocabulary plus SQ-Codec's diffusion-oriented design. **That exception list is contradicted by the paper's own Table 9:** DWavL at C = 6 goes 4.32 → **5.07** on test-clean, DWav2Vec2 at C = 2 goes 4.47 → **8.44**, and on Welsh every semantic tokenizer degrades badly (DWavL-6: 48.94 → **84.01**). The deep-head benefit is real for acoustic tokenizers and unreliable for semantic ones.

### 5.5 Ranking and trade-offs (§5.3–5.4)

Ranks are computed per metric within a task (rank N best of N tokenizers), averaged across metrics, then across tasks in a category. **No tokenizer dominates all categories.** **Discrete WavLM achieves the highest overall ranking on speech; EnCodec performs best on music and general audio** — the latter, again, within a four-way acoustic-only field. The authors explicitly downgrade the radar plots to "a high-level overview of performance trends rather than strict recommendations".

Table 5's qualitative summary: **Acoustic** = high reconstruction, low downstream, requires large models, slow convergence, high efficiency. **Semantic** = low reconstruction, high downstream, can work with smaller models, fast convergence, low efficiency. **Hybrid** = moderate reconstruction and downstream, "depends on data", moderate convergence and efficiency. *(The Low-Resource Robustness column is glyph-only and survives no extraction mode — read it from the rendered PDF before citing that cell.)*

The semantic tokenizers do the reverse.** Speaker similarity after resynthesis sits at **18.3–34.9 across all k-means rows** (DWav2Vec2 at C = 2 is the floor at 18.29) against **78–87 for the codecs at their highest #Q** — though the low-#Q codec rows fall to 34.8–44.9 and WavTokenizer sits at 59.89, so quote the comparison at matched #Q. Appendix F also observes that **increasing the number of codebooks consistently improves reconstruction** in this table while often reducing downstream performance.

This is the empirical payload of the whole paper: *"strong reconstruction quality does not necessarily indicate better preservation of semantic or task-relevant information"*, because reconstruction scores largely reflect **decoder** strength, since that is where the training losses are applied.

### 5.7 Appendix E — embedding initialisation

Random vs encoder-embedding initialisation (pretrained, then fine-tuned — not frozen), on in-domain ASR (BiLSTM head) and out-of-distribution KS (ECAPA-TDNN head), Table 12:

| Model | #Q | ASR clean/other, random | ASR clean/other, init | KS random | KS init |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Enc-SMA-24 | 8 | 16.83 / 38.98 | **10.69 / 28.64** | 68.53 | **76.50** |
| DAC-SMA-24 | 8 | 16.29 / 39.57 | **15.52 / 37.54** | 61.83 | **77.60** |
| ST-S-16 | 8 | 12.69 / 30.99 | **8.56 / 24.44** | **92.69** | 87.60 |
| Mimi-S-24 | 8 | 13.96 / 31.53 | **11.86 / 28.18** | 92.14 | 91.17 |

Read carefully. On **in-domain ASR, encoder initialisation improves every tokenizer in the table, semantic included** (DWavL 4.32 → 3.77). On **out-of-distribution KS, it hurts the semantic and hybrid ones** (DWavL 97.20 → 87.50; ST 92.69 → 87.60; Mimi 92.14 → 91.17) and helps the three acoustic ones.

**The paper's own summary of this table overstates it**, and the sentence is worth quoting because it is a second, cleaner instance of the W1 defect: *"Consistent with findings in (Mousavi et al., 2024), **semantic tokenizers tend to perform better with random initialization**, while acoustic tokenizers improve with encoder-based initialization."* Table 12's ASR column flatly contradicts the first half. The paper does hedge elsewhere — it says its observations only *partially* support its prior hypothesis, and that encoder embeddings are often lower-dimensional (e.g. 128) and need an extra projection to reach 1024-d, so **the gain may come from the added capacity rather than the initialisation**. Headline results use random init everywhere for fairness, with learning rate re-tuned separately to remove optimisation bias.

## 6. Assumptions and limitations

**Stated by the authors**
1. Not aiming for SOTA per task — deliberately simple heads, to measure tokens rather than heads.
2. Off-the-shelf tokenizers only; sampling rate, training data and model scale are acknowledged confounds, declared out of scope (deferred to Mousavi et al. 2025).
3. Rankings are trend indicators, not recommendations.
4. Encoder-init gains may be a capacity artefact (§5.7).
5. Libri2Mix is "largely saturated", so no dedicated strong separation backbone (Conv-TasNet etc.) for speech; stronger baselines are used only for music and general audio.

**Not stated, but real**
6. **No V ablation — and the paper's own signposting implies otherwise.** §2 promises to "analyze factors such as **codebook size** and downstream head design", and §5.2's heading is literally "**Impact of Codebook Size and Bitrate**". Only **C** is ever varied. The one V datapoint (VoxtLM 200 → 1000) is cited, not measured. This is the sharpest limitation in the paper for our purposes.
7. **The continuous baseline is neither one system nor constant across heads.** Besides the six different backbones, the Continuous row itself changes between downstream architectures: ASR-clean **2.26 (Table 2) vs 4.07 (Table 9)**; TTS UTMOS **3.10 vs 4.08**; SS dWER **9.97 vs 24.42**; SV EER **2.10 vs 5.82**; ESC **92.91 vs 94.50**. Any "continuous beats discrete by X" statement is therefore head-specific as well as task-specific. *(Note the scope: §4.2 confirms SE/SS/ASR use the same architecture with WavLM weighted-sum, so those comparisons are controlled; ESC, MGC, MUSDB, FUSS and multi-speaker TTS are not.)*
8. **Only four of nine tokenizers are evaluated outside speech.** Every music/general-audio conclusion is an acoustic-only conclusion.
9. **20 TPE trials × 3 seeds is thin** for configurations this unstable. Several cells have standard deviations that swamp the differences being ranked: `50.84 ± 34.53`, `44.60 ± 39.19`, `56.28 ± 26.46`, `47.17 ± 40.43`. The ranking procedure ingests them anyway.
10. **Only one semantic tokenisation recipe.** All three "semantic" entries are the same Mousavi et al. 2024 procedure (six fixed layers, V = 1000, plain k-means) over three SSL backbones. Layer choice, V and clustering algorithm are all fixed — "semantic tokens behave like X" is really "this one recipe behaves like X".
11. **No significance testing anywhere** — see §10.

## 8. Strengths and weaknesses (pass 3)

**Strengths we can borrow**
- **S1 — The decoder-free protocol is a transferable idea.** Our pose analogue: evaluate pose tokens by what a downstream model can do with them, *not* by how well reconstructed skeletons look. Appendix F is the citation that makes this principled rather than convenient.
- **S2 — Reconstruction/downstream anti-correlation is directly quotable** for framing why a reconstruction-free clustering vocabulary is legitimate.
- **S3 — "Semantic tokens generally outperform acoustic tokens"**, from a peer-reviewed TMLR benchmark, is the strongest external support for our novelty claim in the *speech* domain. It licenses precisely this: **a token family produced by offline clustering of frozen SSL features beats learned VQ codebooks on most speech tasks** — exactly our design. The motion-domain version remains unrun; that is our white space.
- **S4 — Protocol hygiene worth copying wholesale**: two heads per task, ≥3 seeds with std. dev., per-configuration LR tuning.
- **S5 — Eq. (1)** turns "vocabulary size" from an opinion into an accounting identity, and it reproduces the paper's own EnCodec range exactly.

**Weaknesses to name when we cite it**
- **W1 — "Continuous wins across all domains and tasks" is contradicted by the paper's own Table 3** (SE DNSMOS under both heads; TTS under head 1). Appendix E's initialisation sentence has the same defect (§5.7). Cite scoped versions.
- **W2 — The continuous baseline is heterogeneous *and* head-dependent** (§6-7), so cross-task and cross-table gap sizes are not comparable.
- **W3 — The V question is genuinely open in this paper, despite §5.2's heading.** Anyone citing DASB for "small vocabularies are better" is citing VoxtLM through DASB. Cite VoxtLM directly, or write "as reported in DASB citing Maiti et al. (2024)".
- **W4 — Outside speech the field is four acoustic tokenizers**, so "EnCodec is best for music" is a much narrower claim than it looks.
- **W5 — Seed variance sometimes exceeds the effects being ranked**, with no significance testing.
- **W6 — Appendix D's "SQ-Codec is the only exception" is contradicted by Table 9** (§5.4d).
- **W7 — Copy-editing errors throughout** (§0, Clarity). Verify every number against the rendered PDF.

## 9. What this changes for our project

**9.1 The bitrate identity applies to us directly.**
Our vocabulary V = 216 with C = 1 stream. With window/frame rate R, our bitrate is log₂(216) · 1 · R = **7.755 · R bps**. At R = 30 codes/s that is ≈ **233 bps** — roughly **4× below WavTokenizer's 0.9 kbps**, the leanest configuration in the benchmark, and **~100× below DAC at C = 32**. We are nowhere near the over-budget regime DASB shows to be dangerous; we are far under it.

**9.2 E4's sweep is a much weaker intervention than DASB's evidence suggests is needed — worth knowing before spending the compute.**
The proposed sweep V ∈ {60, 87, 120, 216} at fixed R moves log₂V across {5.91, 6.44, 6.91, 7.75} — a **total bitrate range of 1.31×**. DASB's bitrate evidence spans **16× within a tokenizer** (and ~27× across the benchmark), and it is obtained by moving **C**, not V. So:
- The honest framing of E4 is **"does vocabulary granularity matter at fixed, already-low bitrate?"** — a question DASB *signposts in its §5.2 heading but does not answer*.
- The checklist's instruction to report against a log₂(V)·R budget is right, and Eq. (1) is the right instrument. But the expected effect size is small. The analogue of DASB's *large* effect would be sweeping **C** — **multi-stream tokenisation** (separate token streams for upper body / lower body, or a coarse stream plus a residual stream). **Recommend logging this as a candidate E8**; it converges with checklist item #10 (mSTAMP limb-subspace) and #19 (Skeleton Motion Words), which argue for per-limb decomposition from a different direction.
- The checklist's caution "**V = 216 is already in the good zone, don't shrink to 60–100 blindly**" survives verification, but its evidentiary basis is **one cited TTS CER datapoint from VoxtLM**, not DASB data. State it that way in the thesis.

**9.3 DASB supplies a defensible answer to the "why not a learned codebook?" reviewer question.** In the only large, peer-reviewed, unified-protocol comparison that exists (TMLR 2026), offline k-means over frozen self-supervised features outperforms **learned VQ codebooks** on most discriminative and generative *speech* tasks. The learned codebooks' advantage is confined to tasks needing fine-grained identity/timbre and to reconstruction fidelity — which our task does not optimise for.

**9.4 …and it supplies the counter-question we must pre-empt.** Acoustic tokens win **speaker identity** (DAC on SI, SQ-Codec on SV), and k-means tokens lose **two-thirds of speaker similarity** after resynthesis (18–35 vs 78–87 at matched high #Q). The motion analogue is **dancer identity / individual movement signature**. If any part of our evaluation rewards preserving an individual dancer's style, DASB predicts our clustering vocabulary will be **structurally weak** there. This belongs in Limitations, paired with A0-8 and DisCoRD (L3-20).

**9.5 The domain-match risk is NOT supported by this paper — do not cite DASB for it.** My first draft claimed §5.3's music results showed speech-fitted k-means failing on music. That is wrong: **no semantic tokenizer is evaluated on music or general audio at all** (§5.3). The domain-mismatch risk named in checklist item #9 still stands, but it rests on **L3-22 (Labrak et al.) alone**. DASB is silent on it.

**9.6 Protocol items to adopt now** (cheap, high credibility return): report **≥3 seeds with std. dev.**, run **two downstream heads** (our LSTM plus a shallower and a deeper variant), and **tune the learning rate per configuration** rather than once globally. DASB's evidence that discrete-token results swing by ~8 WER points on head depth alone — and by ~35 points for DWavL on Welsh in the wrong direction — means a single-configuration result on our side is not interpretable.

**9.7 E7 is unaffected.** DASB's metrics are all corpus-based and supervised; nothing here displaces the corpus-free metric set (Coverage / LocalDiv / InterDiv / SiFID) from A1-13 / SinMDM — checklist #2, the next paper due.

## ⚠️ สามข้อแก้ให้ checklist (TIER 0 ข้อ 3)

1. **"§5.2 ให้สมการ bitrate"** → สมการนี้คือ **Eq. (1) อยู่ใน §3.2** ไม่ใช่ §5.2 · ถ้าอ่านแค่ §5.2 ตามที่ checklist สั่ง **จะพลาดสมการที่ต้องการทั้งดุ้น**

3. **DASB ไม่เคย sweep V เลย** — ผลเรื่อง bitrate ทุกอันของ DASB เกิดจากการเปลี่ยน **C (จำนวน codebook)** ทั้งหมด ทั้งที่ §2 เขียนว่าจะ "analyze factors such as **codebook size**" และหัวข้อ §5.2 ชื่อว่า "**Impact of Codebook Size and Bitrate**" ตรง ๆ — **คนที่เชื่อหัวข้อจะเข้าใจผิดว่ามีการ sweep V**

## สมการที่ต้องการ — Eq. (1), §3.2

> **bitrate = log₂V · C · R**
> C = จำนวน codebook · V = ขนาด vocabulary ต่อ codebook · R = frame rate (code ต่อวินาที)

**ตรวจสอบแล้วว่าใช้ได้จริง:** §3.1 บอกว่า EnCodec รองรับ 1.5–24 kbps · แทนค่า V=1024 (log₂=10), R=75 → C=2 ได้ **1 500 bps = 1.5 kbps**, C=32 ได้ **24 000 bps = 24 kbps** — **ตรงเป๊ะทั้งสองปลาย** สมการนี้อ้างได้อย่างปลอดภัย

**ช่วงที่ DASB สำรวจจริง:** **16 เท่า ภายใน tokenizer ตัวเดียว** (EnCodec/DAC, C = 2→32, V คงที่) และ **~27 เท่า ทั้ง benchmark** (0.9 → 24 kbps, ซึ่ง V ไม่ถูกคุม) — **ไม่มีตรงไหนที่ตรึง C กับ R แล้วขยับ V เลย**

## ผลต่อโปรเจกต์เรา

**1. สมการ bitrate ใช้กับเราได้ตรง ๆ** — V = 216, C = 1 → bitrate = **7.755 · R bps** · ถ้า R = 30 code/วินาที ได้ ≈ **233 bps** ซึ่ง **ต่ำกว่า WavTokenizer (0.9 kbps) ราว 4 เท่า** และ **ต่ำกว่า DAC ที่ C=32 ราว 100 เท่า** → **เราไม่ได้อยู่ในโซนอันตรายที่ bitrate สูงเกิน เราอยู่ต่ำกว่านั้นมาก**

**2. E4 อ่อนกว่าที่คิด** — sweep V ∈ {60, 87, 120, 216} ที่ R คงที่ ขยับ log₂V จาก 5.91 → 7.75 = **ช่วง bitrate แค่ 1.31 เท่า** เทียบกับ **16 เท่า** ที่ DASB สำรวจ (และสำรวจด้วยการขยับ **C** ไม่ใช่ V)
- กรอบที่ซื่อสัตย์ของ E4 คือ **"ความละเอียดของ vocabulary สำคัญไหม ที่ bitrate ต่ำคงที่?"** — คำถามที่ DASB **ตั้งหัวข้อไว้แต่ไม่ได้ตอบ**
- สิ่งที่เทียบเคียงกับผลแรง ๆ ของ DASB คือการ sweep **C** = **multi-stream tokenisation** (แยก stream บน/ล่างของร่างกาย หรือ stream หยาบ + stream residual) → **เสนอให้บันทึกเป็น E8** · ไปทางเดียวกับ checklist ข้อ #10 (mSTAMP limb-subspace) และ #19 (Skeleton Motion Words)
- คำเตือน "**V=216 อยู่ในโซนดีแล้ว**" **ยังยืนอยู่** แต่ฐานหลักฐานคือ **ตัวเลข CER ตัวเดียวที่อ้างมาจาก VoxtLM** ไม่ใช่ข้อมูลของ DASB → ต้องเขียนแบบนั้นในวิทยานิพนธ์

**3. ตอบคำถาม reviewer "ทำไมไม่ใช้ learned codebook"** — ในการเปรียบเทียบใหญ่ที่สุดที่ผ่าน peer review และใช้ protocol เดียวกัน (TMLR 2026) **k-means แบบ offline บน feature ที่แช่แข็งไว้ ชนะ learned VQ codebook ในงาน speech ส่วนใหญ่** และข้อได้เปรียบของ learned codebook จำกัดอยู่ที่งานที่ต้องการตัวตน/timbre ละเอียด กับความแม่นในการ reconstruct — ซึ่งงานเราไม่ได้ optimise เพื่อสิ่งนั้น

**4. คำถามย้อนกลับที่ต้องกันไว้ล่วงหน้า** — acoustic token ชนะเรื่อง **ตัวตนผู้พูด** · อะนาล็อกฝั่ง motion คือ **ตัวตนของนักเต้น / ลายเซ็นการเคลื่อนไหวเฉพาะบุคคล** · ถ้าส่วนใดของการประเมินให้คะแนนการรักษาสไตล์เฉพาะตัว **DASB ทำนายว่า vocabulary แบบ clustering ของเราจะอ่อนเชิงโครงสร้างตรงนั้น** → ต้องเขียนใน Limitations คู่กับ A0-8 และ DisCoRD (L3-20)

**5. เรื่อง domain match — ห้ามอ้าง DASB** (ดูหัวข้อ ⚠️ ด้านบน) ใช้ L3-22 อย่างเดียว

**6. protocol ที่ควรลอกมาใช้เลย** — รายงาน **≥3 seed พร้อม std** · ใช้ **downstream head 2 แบบ** (LSTM ของเรา + ตื้นกว่าหนึ่ง + ลึกกว่าหนึ่ง) · **จูน learning rate แยกต่อ configuration** · หลักฐานของ DASB ที่ว่าผลแกว่งได้ ~8 จุด WER แค่จากความลึกของ head (และแกว่ง ~35 จุดในทางที่ผิดสำหรับ DWavL บน Welsh) แปลว่า **ผลจาก configuration เดียวของเราตีความไม่ได้**

**7. E7 ไม่กระทบ** — metric ของ DASB อิง corpus และ supervised ทั้งหมด ไม่ได้แทนที่ metric ที่ไม่ต้องมี corpus (Coverage / LocalDiv / InterDiv / SiFID) จาก A1-13 / SinMDM ซึ่งเป็นเปเปอร์ถัดไปที่ต้องอ่าน (checklist ข้อ 2)
