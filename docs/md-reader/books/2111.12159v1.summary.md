>
> **(1) Their pose LSTM is a continuous regressor, not a token classifier. `paper_index_A.csv:45` overstates the match.**

Their network `R` **outputs a pose**: `[f̂ᵗ⁺¹, ĉᵗ⁺¹] = R(nᵗ)` where `f̂ ∈ ℝ³⁺⁴ᴶ` is a root displacement plus 31 unit quaternions, trained with a **geodesic quaternion regression loss** (Eq. 6–8). The discrete vocabulary never enters the LSTM's output space — it enters only as **conditioning** (a 184-D continuous embedding `mᵗ` of the chosen cluster) and as the **sampler above it** (the K=500 transition matrix). So:
> - their **transition matrix** and our `data/features/transition_matrix.npy` *are* the same object — cite them for it;
> - their **LSTM** and our `train_lstm.py` are **not** the same object. **A1-17 (Keyposes, GRU classifying the next cluster label) remains the right precedent for token-classification; A1-16 is the right precedent for the vocabulary + transition matrix + histogram control.** → Split the citation in `paper_index_A.csv`, `paper_chain.md` (R6-2 entry) and `memory.txt:376`.
>
> **(2) The motifs are MEDOIDS — the motion word closest to the k-means centroid under ℓ2 — computed in a *learned* 184-D embedding, not in pose space. The motif level has a hard dependency we do not have.**
> Motion words are mapped through **the pretrained network of Aristidou et al. [14] (*Deep Motifs and Motion Signatures*, ACM TOG 37(6), 2018)** before clustering, and the **motion perceptual loss is L2 in that same frozen embedding** (Eq. 9). Without that encoder there is no motif level and no perceptual loss. Our clustering runs on raw angle/landmark features. This is **the largest single methodological gap between us and them**, and it is also the honest answer to "why don't you just do what A1-16 does".
> ⚠️ **The paper is internally inconsistent on centroid vs medoid.** §3.3 defines the medoid — *"the motion word that is closest to the centroid of the cluster, using ℓ2 distance"* — but §1 says a motif is *"the centroid of a cluster of motion-words"*, the Fig. 2 caption says *"the the centroid of the cluster"* (sic), and §5.1 refers to *"their corresponding centroid"*. The word "medoid" never appears. **§3.3 is the operative definition** (it is the one in the method section), but cite it carefully — and do not treat the other three as a second reading.

> **(3) There is a SECOND K value in this paper that our notes miss: K = 150 for AIST++.** §5.1's headline is K = 500, but §5 also states that for the AIST++ re-training *"in this experiment, we use K = 150 clusters"*, and §6.1 says outright that **"the number of clusters may need to be tweaked for different dance genres or datasets."** That is a second **unswept, corpus-dependent K choice** — evidentially identical in kind to A1-17's 1000→100 cut, and it must be cited with the same caution the A1-17 summary §9-A demands: **a design decision, not an ablation.** Neither paper ever swept k in dance. **Nobody has. That is a real, small, defensible contribution available to us (E4).**
>
> **(4) ⭐ Their chi-square signature distance is a CONFORMITY metric, not a quality metric — and the paper's own Figure 12 shows real motion capture scoring WORSE on it than their synthetic output.**
> `paper_chain.md:1795` promotes *"token histogram per clip + chi-square"* from "queued experiment" to *"method with a citation"* on the strength of this paper. Keep the formula (Eq. 11 is standard and correct), but **never report it alone**, for three compounding reasons:
> - the choreography level's sampler (Eq. 5) is **driven by the signature gap itself**, so Table 1 scores the method on its own objective — the same self-scoring pattern as A1-17's MOAC;
> - the baseline (acLSTM) **has no mechanism to target a signature at all**, so a large gap is a tautology, not a finding;
> - §5.2.4/Fig. 12 state plainly that their outputs *"converge faster and are closer to the target signature than the raw data sample"*. **A real human dance loses to a generator on this metric.** The authors explain it correctly (the template is an average; a real dance is one performer's improvisation) — but that explanation *is* the argument that the metric measures conformity to a mean. A degenerate generator emitting the template histogram scores 0.
> → Our eval plan must pair it with a fidelity term (E7's Coverage/LocalDiv/InterDiv/SiFID) and must report **raw-data chi-square as a reference row**, which this paper does for Fig. 12 but **omits from Table 1**.
> **And the survey says the same thing from the other side:** real mocap **beats** "Ours" on synchronisation (6.27 vs 5.98 non-professionals; 6.73 vs 6.03 professionals) and on realism (67.8 vs 56.7). Ours beats the baseline on all four aspects, but **it "beats" real dance only on richness and on the conformity metric — which §6 argues are the same property measured twice.**
>
> **(5) Eq. (5) is under-specified on three separate counts.** The text says the signature term *"guides the network to generate missing motifs by raising the probability of these motifs"*, but as printed the formula is `p_j^{k+1} = |s_k − s_G| · T_j`, and:
> - **Sign.** The `|·|` is an **absolute** difference. A motif that is *over*-produced has a large `|s_k − s_G|` too, and therefore gets its probability raised as well. With a normalised histogram some bins must overshoot, so this is not a corner case.
> - **Product type.** Both operands are in `ℝᴷ`. A "·" between two `ℝᴷ` vectors is a **scalar**, which cannot be a distribution over the next motif. The only workable reading is an **elementwise** product `⊙`, which is what this summary writes throughout — but that is our reconstruction, not the paper's notation.
> - **Normalisation.** No step is stated that makes `p` sum to 1, although the next motif is *"randomly drawing the index ... from a probability distribution"*.
> If we implement this (E-new-2 below), **use a signed, clipped deficit `max(s_G − s_k, 0)`, elementwise, renormalised** — and say in the write-up that we did and why.

## 1. Problem and motivation (§1–§2)

The framing is that dance has **two scales**, and the field only solves one. Locally: *"motions must be continuous, smooth and expressive"*. Globally: a dance *"has a meaningful global temporal structure"*, *"a structure, a theme, a culture"*. Existing audio-driven work is enumerated and dismissed on exactly this axis — transition graphs synchronised to beat [5], [6], [69] or emotion [8], [19]; HMMs [9]; Gaussian processes [70]; RNNs [10], [11], [12], [71], [72]; autoregressive encoder-decoders [73], [74]; graph-based [75], [76], [77]; transformers [80], [81]. The verdict on all of them: *"all of these methods do not take into consideration the global content of the dance movement."*

The two nearest relatives get individual treatment:
- **ChoreoMaster** [78] — *"created a unified embedding space for music and dance clips, that is fed to a choreography-oriented graph-based motion synthesis framework."* (Useful: our checklist flags L3-26 ChoreoMaster as paywalled, citable only from its abstract. **This one-sentence characterisation from a TVCG-track paper is a citable second-hand description.**)
- **ChoreoNet** [85] — *"while their approach is two-stage, bottom-to-top approach, which includes: (1) generating independent small motion units and (2) stitching them together through (inpainting), it does not takes into consideration the global choreographic structure; our approach is 3-level top-to-bottom."*

§2.5 also notes that choreography *"has only been considered as a high level representation of the moves of multiple characters in the space [82], [83], [84] rather than generating a new dance variation, driven by an input music."*

**The delta against acLSTM [13] is stated explicitly** in §2.5 — three differences: **(a)** rotational rather than positional data, *"to avoid pose ambiguities that is critical for highly dynamic and complex movements"*; **(b)** three extra conditioning signals — audio (4-D), motif (184-D), foot contacts (2-D); **(c)** the motion perceptual loss.

## 2. Data representation (§3) — read this before §4

### 2.1 Audio (§3.1, Appendix Table 2)

Two groups, both from Librosa [60], [86]:

| Group | Dim | Contents | Sampling |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rhythmic** `a_r` | **4** | `a₁` tempogram (local autocorrelation of onset-strength envelope); `a₂` spectral-flux onset-strength envelope; `a₃` RMS value *"to approximate downbeats"*; `a₄` beat rate (binary form) | **per frame**, right-anchored moving window of **10 frames**, step 1 frame |
| **Spectral** `a_s` | **87** (`a₅`–`a₉₁`) | chromagram (12), constant-Q chromagram (12), chroma-energy-normalised (12), mel-scaled spectrogram (26), MFCC (13), spectral centroid / bandwidth / contrast / flatness / roll-off (5), tonal centroid (6), zero-crossing rate (1) | **per window between two consecutive beats** |

*(Arithmetic check, ours: 12+12+12+26+13+1+1+1+1+1+6+1 = 87 = a₅…a₉₁. ✓ Consistent.)*

Beats are *"temporal binary signals"* detected from onset detection plus *"the study of repetitions to determine which onsets constitutes the actual beats"* [87]. **Beat detection is load-bearing** — it defines the motion-word boundaries, so every downstream unit inherits its errors. The paper never measures beat-detection accuracy.

§3.1.2 explicitly declines STFT and CWT: *"we only use the features integrated in the Librosa library as we noticed that they add enough variation to the outputs."* This is an admitted unswept choice.

### 2.2 Pose (§3.2)

`J = 31` joints, **unit quaternions in hierarchical order** `f_q ∈ ℝ⁴ᴶ = ℝ¹²⁴`, plus root **displacement from the previous frame** `f_r^t ∈ ℝ³`. So `f^t = [f_r^t, f_q] ∈ ℝ³⁺⁴ᴶ = ℝ¹²⁷`. Root-as-displacement *"works well with cycling and repetitive motions, and allows smooth motion synthesis."*

Antipodal handling follows QuaterNet [42]: *"we choose among f_q and −f_q the representation with the lowest Euclidean distance from the representation in the previous frame."*

### 2.3 Motif (§3.3) — ⭐ the section that matters most to us

A **motion word** `w ∈ ℝᴺ×|f|` is *"a collection of N consecutive skeleton poses"*. Four design decisions, each one a decision we also face:

| Decision | A1-16 | Verbatim |
|---|---|---|
| **Segmentation** | **On the beat**, not fixed-window | *"motion sequences are divided into motion words on the beat"* |
| **Overlap** | **None** | *"there is no overlap between two consecutive motion words"* — stated flatly. (Unlike [14], which §2.5 describes as using *"small overlapping movements"*; the contrast the paper draws **explicitly** with [14] is about word *length*, not overlap: *"In contrast to the original paper where motion words have a fixed number of frames, in our case they have different length due to the temporally-variant beat rate."*) |
| **Length normalisation** | Time-scale all words to **13 frames** | *"given the longest word, we time-scale the motion words to have a uniform size of 13 frames (which is 140bpm @30fps)"* |
| **Embedding** | **d = 184**, via the pretrained net of [14] | *"map all motions words extracted from the group into a d-dimensional universal feature space ℝᵈ similar to [14], but use a smaller dimension d = 184 to create a more compact representation"* |

Then: *"We then cluster the motion words using K-means clustering into **K = 500** clusters."* Each cluster `Mᵢ` is represented by `mᵢ ∈ ℝᵈ` = **the embedding of the motion word closest to the centroid under ℓ2** — a **medoid**, not the centroid itself.

**The justification for K = 500 is qualitative and unswept** (§5.1): *"Experiments show that this number is sufficient to represent a diversity of movements within each cluster, while keeping the clusters compact with small mean error between motion words and their corresponding centroid."* No sweep, no elbow plot, no reported mean error value.

One further claim, also unmeasured: *"We have found that segmenting dance motion sequences on the beat assists in creating more compact and better separated motion clusters."* No compactness or separation number is given for the fixed-window alternative. **This is exactly the comparison our E-new-3 below would make, and it is stated as a result without evidence.**

### 2.4 Choreography (§3.4)

Each dance is a **bag-of-motifs**: the **motion signature** `s ∈ [0,1]ᴷ`, a normalised histogram over the K motifs. Stated properties: *"succinct but descriptive"*, *"semantically meaningful"*, *"time-scale invariant and agnostic to the motif order."*

**Genre templates are built by averaging**: *"We create a combined signature templates ... for a specific genre by averaging many dances."* Fig. 3 shows salsa-leader and salsa-follower signatures — *"the dances share many motion words, but with different frequency of appearance"* — which is the paper's own demonstration that **the histogram, not the vocabulary, carries the style**.

## 3. The three levels (§4)

### 3.1 Pose level — `R` (§4.1)

An acLSTM [13]: **three fully connected layers, memory size 1024**, auto-conditioned (fed its own outputs during training so it stays stable at test time). Input at time `t`:

```
nᵗ = [a_rᵗ, mᵗ, fᵗ, cᵗ]            (1)
[f̂ᵗ⁺¹, ĉᵗ⁺¹] = R(nᵗ)              (2)
```

with `a_rᵗ ∈ ℝ⁴`, `mᵗ ∈ ℝ¹⁸⁴`, `fᵗ ∈ ℝ¹²⁷`, `cᵗ ∈ {0,1}²`. *(Derived, ours: the input vector is **317-D**. The paper never states this total.)*

Foot contacts are extracted in preprocessing *"by examining the distance of the foot joints from the ground and their velocity"* (no threshold given). Predicting them *"by itself, with no further processing, improves the quality of our outputs"*, but *"to completely remove foot sliding artifacts, we also use standard Inverse Kinematic (IK) optimization, on corrected foot positions, as a post-processing step."*

### 3.2 Motif level — `H` (§4.2)

On every beat `k`, the `N_k` generated poses since the last beat form a motion word `ŵ_k`. Then:

```
ŵ_{k+1}, ĉ^w_{k+1} = H(w_k, c_w^k, m_{k+1}, a_{k+1})     (3)
```

*"where H(·) is Nₖ compositions of R(·) ... and m_{k+1} and a_{k+1} remain constant per motion word."* **The motif and audio conditioning are held fixed for the whole word** — this is what makes a word a unit rather than a running average.

**AdaIN style injection (§4.2.1).** An MLP maps `a_s^k ∈ ℝ⁸⁷` → **248 parameters** (= 2 × 124, i.e. a scale and a shift *per joint quaternion*), tiled along time to `d_k ∈ ℝ²⁴⁸ˣᴺ`, split into `d_k^(1)`, `d_k^(2) ∈ ℝ¹²⁴ˣᴺ`. Joints are treated as channels. Temporal coherence between words is preserved by a **Hamming window** `γ(t) ∈ ℝᴺ` centred on the middle frame, *"decays towards its edges (the first and last frames of the motion word remain unmodified)"*:

```
w̃_k = (1 + β × γ(t) × d_k^(1)) ŵ_k + β × γ(t) × d_k^(2)      (4)
```

⚠️ **The parenthesisation above is our reconstruction, not the paper's typography.** All four extraction modes lose the brackets (`-raw` gives `wk = 1 + β × γ(t) × dk (1) ŵk + β × γ(t) × dk (2)`). Grouping the scale term is the only sensible AdaIN reading, but flag it as reconstructed if it is ever quoted.

**`β = 40`** — *"a scalar that controls the amount of variation we apply"*. No sensitivity analysis. ⚠️ Recovered only with `-enc UTF-8`; the default extraction prints the equation with the symbol missing.

**Motion perceptual loss (§4.2.2).** The stated motivation: *"there are no large-scale pretrained motion classification networks, and it is challenging to semantically label a dataset of short movements to train a motion classification network."* So instead of an activity-recognition net (which is what Ren et al. [92] used on 2D pose), they use `P` from [14], *"trained to distinguish between groups of similar motions disregarding low level details, temporal warping and frame rate differences."* The stated reason this is *better* here than an activity-recognition net is worth quoting in full, because it is an argument we can reuse for our own token features:

> *"the extracted features to contain information related to the spatio-temporal nature of the movement itself (e.g., "right arm is raised up"), while such information will be "cleaned" by deep features extracted from an activity recognition that tries to recognize the high-level semantic meaning of the motion (e.g.,"waving hands")."*

### 3.3 Choreography level (§4.3)

Two criteria combine:
1. **Motif transition matrix `T`** — *"describes probability of the temporal connectivity between consecutive motion motifs for a given dance genre ... computed by counting appearances of each pair in real dances in the genre."* A plain first-order Markov count matrix over K=500 ids. **This is our `transition_matrix.npy`, at word granularity.**
2. **Signature difference** — the running signature `s_k` versus the genre target `s_G`.

```
p_j^{k+1} = |s_k − s_G| ⊙ T_j        (5)
```

`T_j ∈ ℝᴷ` is the transition row from the current motif `j`. The next motif index is drawn randomly from `p`. ⚠️ See READ-THIS-FIRST (5): the absolute value contradicts the stated intent, and no renormalisation is specified.

*"The selected motif m_{k+1} is then set as 'target' input to the acLSTM network."* **This sampler is the entire source of the paper's multi-modality** — §5.2.3: *"the standard acLSTM always synthesizes the same dance ... This is due to the controlled randomness in the selection of the target motifs by the choreography level."*

### 3.4 Losses (§4.4)

Pose loss is the geodesic quaternion distance of Lee et al. [20]:

```
d_q(f_j, f_i) = Σ_{l=1..J} ‖ log( q_{j,l}^{-1} q_{i,l} ) ‖₂        (6)
```

plus *"a forward kinematic layer that is applied to the output rotations [42] ... an additional loss on positional data"*, to *"mitigate error accumulation of joint rotations along the kinematic chain"*.

Auto-conditioning gives two temporal-coherence terms — one when the network is fed ground truth (7), one when fed its own output (8) — and the perceptual term:

```
L_perc = ‖ P(H(w_k, m_{k+1})) − m_{k+1} ‖₂                          (9)
L = L_coh + L_ac_coh + λ L_perc,     λ = 0.5                        (10)
```

⚠️ `λ` is another glyph the default extraction drops.

**Training:** sequences of **100 consecutive frames**, **500,000 iterations**, **Adam** [93], **lr = 0.0001**, **batch 32**. **~8 hours** on one NVIDIA Titan XP (8-core Intel i7-6850K @ 3.6 GHz, 32 GB RAM). PyTorch. **Synthesis is real-time.** At inference the network is primed with **15 frames** plus the corresponding motif and foot contacts.

## 6. ⭐ The metric problem — the most important thing in this read

Everything in §5.1 and Fig. 12 turns on one metric:

```
d_s(s_i, s_j) = ½ Σ_{k=1..K} (s_ik − s_jk)² / (s_ik + s_jk)        (11)
```

Standard chi-square distance on normalised histograms. The formula is correct and `paper_chain.md:2163` records it correctly. **The problem is not the formula, it is what it measures.**

**Three compounding issues, in increasing order of importance:**

**(a) It is the choreography level's objective.** Eq. (5) samples motifs *in proportion to the signature gap*. Table 1 then measures the signature gap. This is A1-17's MOAC pattern exactly: the paper introduces both the mechanism and the metric it wins on. It is not fraud — it is a legitimate demonstration that the control mechanism controls what it claims to control — but it is **not evidence of dance quality**, and Table 1 is the only quantitative table in the paper.

**(b) The baseline cannot compete by construction.** acLSTM has no signature target, no transition matrix and no sampler. Reporting that it scores 0.378 against a target it was never given is not a comparison. The **`Ours (no L_perc)` column is the meaningful one**, and its margin is much smaller: on Modern, 0.337 → 0.323, a **4.2%** improvement on n = 3 examples with no variance reported.

**(c) ⭐ Real motion capture loses.** §5.2.4/Fig. 12: *"all of the samples generated by our method (red shaded colors) converge faster and are closer to the target signature than the raw data sample (black color)."* The paper's own explanation is correct and is also the indictment: *"This because the target signature is the average signature over all the data sample, while specific dances are based on the improvisation of a specific performer."*

**So the metric rewards regression to the corpus mean.** Its global minimum (0) is attained by a generator that emits the template histogram and nothing else — which is to say, by a *maximally average* dancer. Combined with the richness result (§5.4: ours 6.91 > raw 6.35, because the sampler covers more of the vocabulary than any one performer does), the picture is consistent: **this system produces a genre-average dance, and both of its favourable numbers are measuring that same property.**

**What this changes for us.** `paper_chain.md:1795` and `:2517` list "token histogram per clip + chi-square" as a method with a citation. Keep it — but:
1. **Always report a raw-data reference row**, which Table 1 omits and Fig. 12 supplies. If our generated clips beat real clips on it, say so and say why.
2. **Never report it without a fidelity term.** E7's corpus-free metrics (Coverage / LocalDiv / InterDiv / SiFID from SinMDM, checklist #2) are the right pairing: chi-square measures *"does it use the right movements in the right proportion"*, SiFID/LocalDiv measure *"is each movement any good"*. Neither alone is a quality metric.
3. **Frame it as a control metric, not an evaluation metric.** Its honest use is the one A1-16 puts it to in Fig. 12: showing convergence of a controlled process over time. That is a legitimate and citable use.

## 7. Assumptions and limitations

### 7.1 The paper's own (§6.1)

1. *"our training motion captured data has some artifacts, mainly due to errors during data acquisition, that also affect the quality of the synthesized motion."*
2. *"Other acoustic features, such as the volume, melody, or different instruments could also be taken into consideration."*
3. **⭐** *"The number of clusters that define the motifs plays an important role in synthesis, more particularly it affects the convergence of the network, which is highly depended in the training dataset. ... This means that the **number of clusters may need to be tweaked for different dance genres or datasets**."*
4. **⭐** Inherited from [14]: *"another limitation incurs on the building of the signature where all motion words are clustered to the closest motifs. In that manner, **outliers cannot be correctly assigned to their representative cluster**, thus the accuracy of the signature created is reduced; in addition, it makes clustering **prone to averaging effects**, especially if the diversity of the motion words is large, or the outlier word is far away from the centroid."* They also report *"a number of outliers in the embedding space mainly due to erratic motions in the training data."*
5. *"it cannot replace the choreographer's creativity, which includes artistic concepts such as spontaneity, improvisation, experience, and talent."*

**Limitation 4 is a hard-assignment critique of k-means written by k-means users, and it is the best argument in the literature for our density-based (HDBSCAN) tokenizer**, which has an explicit noise label instead of forcing outliers into a cluster. Quote it.

### 9-B. White space: **SURVIVES**, and here is the precise boundary

Verified against the full text, not a summary. A1-16:
- clusters **motion words** (sub-second, beat-length) — **not whole-dance structure curves**;
- builds genre templates by **averaging** signatures of dances whose genre labels are **given** — not by clustering dances to **discover** structural types;
- **imposes** the target structure top-down at generation time (Eq. 5).

Our Phase-2 question — *do recurring structural types exist across a corpus, discoverable without genre labels?* — is not asked anywhere in this paper. **The white space holds against its strongest test so far.**

⚠️ **But the framing has to be precise, because a reviewer will push here.** Averaging signatures per genre *is* a supervised cousin of what we propose to do unsupervised. The defensible sentence is: *"A1-16 verifies that a target structural distribution can be enforced given a known genre label; it does not ask whether such structural classes can be recovered from the corpus itself."* Do not write "nobody has looked at global structure" — they have, and they published it.

## 13.6 ข้อจำกัด

**เปเปอร์ยอมรับเอง (§6.1):** ข้อมูล mocap มี artifact · ยังไม่ได้ใช้ volume/melody/เครื่องดนตรี · ⭐ **"จำนวนคลัสเตอร์มีบทบาทสำคัญ … อาจต้องปรับตามแนวเต้นหรือชุดข้อมูล"** · ⭐ **สืบทอดจาก [14]: "outlier ไม่สามารถถูกจัดเข้าคลัสเตอร์ตัวแทนได้ถูกต้อง … ทำให้ clustering มีแนวโน้มเกิด averaging effect"** · แทนที่ความคิดสร้างสรรค์ของนักออกแบบท่าเต้นไม่ได้

> **ข้อจำกัดข้อที่ 4 คือคำวิจารณ์ hard assignment ของ k-means ที่เขียนโดยคนที่ใช้ k-means เอง และเป็นข้อโต้แย้งภายนอกที่แข็งที่สุดสำหรับ tokenizer แบบ density-based (HDBSCAN) ของเรา** ซึ่งมี noise label ชัดเจนแทนที่จะยัด outlier เข้าคลัสเตอร์ — **ยกมาอ้างได้เลย**

**ที่เราเพิ่ม (เปเปอร์ไม่ได้บอก):** ไม่เทียบกับระบบคู่แข่งที่ตีพิมพ์แล้วเลย — baseline เชิงปริมาณตัวเดียวคือ acLSTM ซึ่งก็คือ pose level ของตัวเองที่ถอด 2 ชั้นบนออก · *(เปเปอร์ให้เหตุผลไว้เองใน §5.2 และเป็นเหตุผลที่ยุติธรรม: "การสร้างแอนิเมชันเต้นตามดนตรีเป็นหัวข้อที่ค่อนข้างใหม่ … ส่วนใหญ่เพราะขาดชุดข้อมูลเสียง-ท่าเต้นที่จับคู่กัน" → รับเหตุผลได้ แต่ไม่รับ Table 1 ในฐานะ benchmark)* · **เทรนแนวละ 1 run ไม่มี seed และไม่รายงานการกระจายตัวที่ไหนเลย** (ค่าใน Table 1 และอัตราใน §5.4.1 เป็น *ค่าเฉลี่ย* ตามที่คำบรรยายเขียนเอง แต่ไม่มี SD/CI/significance test) · n = 3 สำหรับ 2 ใน 4 แถวของ Table 1 · **ไม่เคย sweep K ทั้ง 500 และ 150** · β/λ/d/13 เฟรม/15 เฟรม ไม่มี sensitivity analysis · **ไม่เคยวัดความแม่นของ beat detection** ทั้งที่ทุกขอบเขต motion word ขึ้นกับมัน · สมการ (5) ใช้ `|·|` ขัดเจตนา + มิติผิด + ไม่ normalise · **94.5% เป็นไปไม่ได้ทางเลขคณิต** · ความสมจริงไม่ระบุจำนวนการตัดสินต่อเงื่อนไข · 2 ใน 4 แนวเต้นยอมรับว่าเทรนไม่พอ · **ไม่ปล่อยอะไรเลยทั้ง 17 หน้า** (URL เดียวในเอกสารคือ Wikipedia ref [1] กับ DOI ใน ref [71]) · ข้ออ้างว่าตัดตามจังหวะให้คลัสเตอร์ "กระชับและแยกกันดีกว่า" **ไม่มีการวัดรองรับ** · **ขนาดตัวอย่างจริงของคลังคำเล็กกว่าที่คิด** เพราะตัดคำแบบ **ไม่ซ้อนทับ** (~3 คำ/วินาที) ต่างจาก sliding window ของเราที่ได้ตัวอย่างต่อชั่วโมงมากกว่ามาก — สำคัญเวลาเทียบ "เขาใช้ K=500 ทำไมเราใช้ 216–362"
